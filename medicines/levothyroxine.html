Levothyroxine

Eltroxin

Thyroid hormone replacement

Key points

  • Replaces the thyroid hormone your body is not making enough of. Used for an underactive thyroid.
  • Usually taken once a day on an empty stomach, 30 to 60 minutes before the first meal, caffeine or other medicines.
  • Usually long term. Blood tests are used to check the dose.
  • Most side effects happen when the dose is too high.

The details

How it works

Levothyroxine is a replacement for thyroxine, the main hormone made by the thyroid gland. These hormones set the speed of the body's metabolism. In the body it is converted to the more active tri-iodothyronine.

How quickly

The SmPC says the half-life in the blood is about a week, so the effect builds slowly. Doses are changed in steps every 3 to 4 weeks, and blood tests guide the dose.

Doses

The SmPC says adults under 50 usually start on 100 micrograms a day, adjusted by 50 micrograms every 3 to 4 weeks, with a final dose of 100 to 200 micrograms. Over 50 without heart disease, the start is no more than 50 micrograms. With heart disease, the start is 25 micrograms a day or 50 on alternate days. NHS says to take it once a day at the same time, on an empty stomach. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

Most side effects happen when the dose is too high: trouble sleeping, headaches and feeling anxious. They often improve when the dose is changed.

Get help if: side effects bother you. Your dose is set by a doctor using blood tests, so do not adjust it yourself. See the NHS page.

Extra care
  • Not for people with an overactive thyroid. Tell a doctor about diabetes, adrenal conditions or a heart condition.
  • Extra care (SmPC 4.4): people over 50, long-standing hypothyroidism, heart disease or high blood pressure, adrenal or pituitary problems (a corticosteroid may be needed first), and epilepsy.
  • Diabetes: doses of insulin or metformin may need changing. Too high a dose can raise the risk of heart rhythm problems and bone loss, so the lowest effective dose is used.
  • Other medicines: NHS lists carbamazepine, omeprazole and other acid reducers, semaglutide, sertraline, antacids, calcium and iron, beta blockers, digoxin and St John's wort.
  • Serious but rare: allergic reaction and thyroid crisis. See the NHS page.
Common mistakes
  • Taking it with breakfast, coffee, or alongside iron, calcium or antacids. NHS says to take it 30 to 60 minutes before.
  • Changing the dose yourself. Blood tests are used to set it.
  • Stopping because you feel well. It is usually for life.
  • Taking biotin supplements without telling the lab or doctor. NHS says biotin can affect thyroid tests.
  • Doubling up after a missed dose.
Pregnancy

NHS says levothyroxine can be taken in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and that more blood tests may be needed in pregnancy. The SmPC says its safety in pregnancy is not known but any risk should be weighed against untreated hypothyroidism, and that only low amounts pass into breast milk.
