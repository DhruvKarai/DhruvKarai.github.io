Aspirin

Antiplatelet and painkiller

NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug) that also makes platelets less sticky

Key points

  • At a low daily dose, aspirin lowers the chance of a blood clot, so it is used to help prevent heart attacks and strokes.
  • At a higher dose, taken only when needed, it relieves pain.
  • Do not give aspirin to children aged 15 or under. It can cause a rare but serious condition called Reye's syndrome.
  • It can cause stomach ulcers and bleeding. Check first if you have had a stomach ulcer, or have asthma, or kidney, liver or heart failure.

The details

How it works

Aspirin blocks an enzyme in platelets called cyclo-oxygenase, so the platelets make less thromboxane A2. Thromboxane A2 helps platelets activate and stick together to form clots. The SmPC says this block is irreversible, because platelets have no nucleus and cannot make new enzyme. Aspirin is also an NSAID, so at higher doses it relieves pain.

How quickly

The SmPC says aspirin is rapidly absorbed from the gut. Low-dose aspirin is taken every day to lower the long-term chance of a clot, not for quick pain relief. NHS says many people take it for a long time, sometimes for life.

Doses

Low dose: NHS says 75mg tablets, usually 1 tablet once a day. For pain: 300mg tablets, 1 to 3 at a time, ideally with or after food, at least 4 hours apart, with no more than 4 doses in a day. Swallow tablets whole with water, or let dispersible tablets dissolve in water first. Do not start long-term aspirin without a doctor's advice. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists indigestion, stomach aches and bleeding more easily than usual as common effects.

Get help if: you have signs of stomach bleeding (tummy pain, pale skin, vomiting blood, or black, sticky, tar-like poo), shortness of breath or wheezing, or signs of a serious allergic reaction.

Extra care
  • Stomach: NHS says aspirin may not be suitable if you have, or have had, a stomach ulcer.
  • Asthma, heart failure, liver or kidney conditions, and blood-clotting problems: NHS says check first.
  • Other medicines: NHS says aspirin may not mix well with blood thinners such as warfarin, some steroids, some antidepressants, some blood pressure medicines, methotrexate, and other NSAIDs such as ibuprofen.
  • Alcohol: NHS says limit alcohol, because it can increase the risk of side effects.
Common mistakes
  • Giving it to a child aged 15 or under.
  • Taking it together with ibuprofen or another NSAID.
  • Stopping a daily low dose without asking the doctor who advised it.
  • Taking two doses to make up for a missed one. NHS says skip it if the next dose is nearly due.
Pregnancy

NHS says to tell your doctor if you are pregnant or get pregnant while taking low-dose aspirin. You can usually keep taking it up to week 20, and sometimes later if your doctor recommends it. A doctor may advise low-dose aspirin from 12 weeks to prevent pre-eclampsia. Higher-dose aspirin for pain is not recommended at any stage of pregnancy, and NHS says paracetamol is usually the best painkiller. For breastfeeding, check with your doctor before taking low-dose aspirin. A higher dose for pain is not usually recommended.
