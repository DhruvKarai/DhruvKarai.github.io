Gabapentin

Anti-epilepsy and nerve pain medicine

Anticonvulsant (anti-epilepsy medicine) also used for nerve pain

Key points

  • Used for epilepsy and for nerve pain, such as pain after shingles or from diabetes.
  • NHS says it is thought to reduce abnormal electrical activity in the brain and to change how pain messages travel.
  • It takes at least a few weeks to work fully.
  • Some people can become addicted. Do not stop suddenly. Your doctor will reduce the dose gradually.

The details

How it works

Gabapentin enters the brain and binds with high affinity to the alpha-2-delta subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels. The SmPC says this may be involved in its anti-seizure and pain-relieving effects, and that it does not bind to GABA receptors or alter GABA metabolism. The exact way it works is not fully understood, so this is a proposed explanation.

How quickly

NHS says it takes at least a few weeks for gabapentin to work. You may still have seizures or pain during this time.

Doses

Your prescriber chooses the dose and builds it up gradually. NHS says capsules contain 100mg, 300mg or 400mg and tablets contain 600mg or 800mg. The liquid usually contains 50mg in 1ml. Do not stop suddenly. When you stop, the dose needs to be reduced gradually to avoid withdrawal symptoms. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS says most people get no side effects, but some feel sleepy, tired or dizzy. NHS also lists feeling or being sick, diarrhoea, mood changes, swollen arms and legs, blurred vision, dry mouth, weight gain and headaches. Common effects are usually mild and go away by themselves.

Get help if: you have a high temperature with swollen glands that do not go away, yellow skin or eyes, unusual bruising or bleeding, long-lasting stomach pain, or you see or hear things that are not there (hallucinations). NHS says talk to your doctor if you are worried about addiction or want to stop.

Extra care
  • Driving: NHS says do not drive, cycle or use machines if you feel sleepy or dizzy. If you have epilepsy there are also driving rules.
  • Alcohol: NHS says you can drink alcohol, but it may make you sleepy. Consider avoiding it for the first few days.
  • Opioids and recreational drugs: NHS says gabapentin can intensify the highs of drugs such as heroin and other opioids.
  • Addiction and withdrawal: NHS says some people become addicted. Talk to your doctor if you are worried.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping suddenly, which can cause withdrawal symptoms.
  • Increasing the dose without asking your doctor.
  • Driving before you know how it affects you.
  • Expecting quick relief. It takes a few weeks.
Pregnancy

NHS says gabapentin is not generally recommended in pregnancy because there is not enough information, though there is no clear evidence that it is harmful. It should only be taken if the benefits outweigh the risks. If you take it for epilepsy, keep taking it and talk to your doctor urgently, because seizures can harm you and your baby. NHS recommends high-dose folic acid (5mg a day) if you are trying to get pregnant. NHS says you can take it while breastfeeding if your baby is healthy, after talking to your doctor or pharmacist, because only very small amounts pass into breast milk.
