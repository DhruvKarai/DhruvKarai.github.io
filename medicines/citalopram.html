Citalopram

SSRI antidepressant

SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor): a type of antidepressant

Key points

  • An antidepressant from the SSRI group. Prescription only.
  • Used to treat depression and panic disorder.
  • It can take a few weeks before you notice an improvement. Keep taking it as your doctor advises.
  • Do not stop it suddenly. Withdrawal symptoms can happen, so your doctor will reduce the dose gradually.

The details

How it works

Citalopram is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI). NHS says it is thought to work by increasing serotonin, a chemical in the brain linked to mood. The SmPC says it is a potent inhibitor of serotonin uptake, with no or minimal effect on noradrenaline, dopamine and GABA uptake.

How quickly

NHS says you may need to take citalopram for a few weeks before you notice an improvement. The SmPC says improvement generally starts after one week but may only be clear from the second week.

Doses

NHS says take it once a day, morning or evening, with or without food, swallowing tablets whole. Your doctor may start with a low dose and increase it after a few weeks. The SmPC says the dose for a major depressive episode in adults is 20mg once a day, which may be raised to a maximum of 40mg. For depression you will usually need at least 6 months of treatment. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists headaches, feeling or being sick, sweating, dry mouth, diarrhoea or constipation, problems sleeping, a fast heartbeat (palpitations) and sexual problems as common effects. Most ease after a couple of weeks.

Get help if: you have thoughts about harming yourself or feel very low, you have signs of serotonin syndrome (fast heartbeat, sweating, shaking, twitching muscles, confusion or agitation), unexplained bruising or bleeding, or signs of a serious allergic reaction. NHS says if you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help straight away.

Extra care
  • Heart conditions: NHS says tell your doctor if you have had a heart attack or have a heart rhythm problem. Citalopram can rarely cause a dangerous irregular heartbeat.
  • Diabetes, epilepsy, liver or kidney disease, or glaucoma: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Bleeding disorders and past mania: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Other medicines: NHS says sumatriptan, strong painkillers such as tramadol, NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, and omeprazole or esomeprazole may not mix well.
  • Under 18s: NHS says it is not usually recommended.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping suddenly because you feel better.
  • Taking two doses to make up for a missed one. NHS says an extra dose can be dangerous.
  • Taking ibuprofen or another NSAID without checking.
  • Giving up in the first week or two. It can take a few weeks.
Pregnancy

NHS says citalopram can be used in pregnancy if needed. Your doctor will talk through risks and benefits, prescribe the lowest effective dose, and may advise giving birth in hospital so you and the baby can be monitored. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant. NHS says citalopram can be used while breastfeeding, though it passes into breast milk in small amounts. Check with your doctor.
