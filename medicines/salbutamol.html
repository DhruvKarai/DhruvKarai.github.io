Salbutamol

Ventolin

Beta-2 agonist: short-acting reliever (bronchodilator)

Key points

  • A reliever inhaler (often blue). It relaxes the muscles of the airways so breathing gets easier.
  • Used when you have symptoms or before something that triggers them, such as exercise.
  • You should feel a difference within a few minutes.
  • Needing it more than usual can be a sign your asthma is not controlled. Talk to a doctor, pharmacist or nurse.

The details

How it works

Salbutamol is a selective beta-2 agonist. It switches on beta-2 receptors in the airway muscles, which relax so the airways open wider. It starts working within about five minutes and lasts around four to six hours.

How quickly

The SmPC says it works within 5 minutes and lasts about 4 to 6 hours. NHS says you should feel a difference within a few minutes.

Doses

NHS says the usual dose for adults and children is 1 or 2 puffs when needed, up to a maximum of 4 times in 24 hours. In a sudden asthma attack, up to 10 puffs can be used, 1 puff at a time with 30 to 60 seconds between puffs. Call 999 if you do not feel better after 10 puffs or you feel worse. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

Feeling shaky, a faster heartbeat that settles quickly, and headaches.

Get help if: you get muscle weakness or cramps, an irregular or racing heartbeat that does not settle, or severe dizziness. For new or worse breathlessness or chest pain call 999 or go to A&E.

Extra care
  • Tell a doctor first about heart problems, an overactive thyroid, low potassium, diabetes or an allergy to salbutamol.
  • If you use too much you may notice a fast heartbeat and feel shaky. Call 999 if you also have chest pain.
  • Other medicines: NHS lists beta blockers, diuretics, steroids, theophylline or aminophylline, and some antidepressants.
  • A serious allergic reaction is rare but possible.
Common mistakes
  • Relying on it as the only treatment. Asthma usually also needs a daily preventer inhaler.
  • Using it more than 4 times in 24 hours, 3 or more days a week, or at night once a week, without telling a healthcare professional.
  • Poor inhaler technique. NHS says to have it checked at least once a year.
  • Not shaking a pressurised inhaler, or not using a spacer when advised.
  • Running out. NHS says to keep a spare.
Pregnancy

NHS says salbutamol can be used in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and that well-treated asthma matters. The SmPC for one product says its safety in pregnancy has not been established and it should only be used if clearly necessary. Follow the advice of your doctor or midwife.
