Prednisolone

Corticosteroid

Key points

  • A steroid medicine that reduces inflammation and calms the immune system.
  • Used for a wide range of problems, including allergies, blood disorders, skin diseases and inflammation, and to prevent organ rejection after a transplant.
  • NHS says take it once a day in the morning, with breakfast. Do not stop suddenly after long use or high doses.
  • If you take it for more than 3 weeks, or on a high dose, you will get a blue steroid card. Carry it.

The details

How it works

Prednisolone is a synthetic glucocorticoid, a copy of the steroid hormones the body makes naturally. The SmPC says synthetic glucocorticoids are used mainly for their strong anti-inflammatory effects in many organ systems. They also have wide metabolic effects and change the immune response to many triggers. Because the effects are so wide, steroids can also cause many side effects.

How quickly

How soon it helps depends on the condition being treated, so your prescriber will tell you what to expect. NHS says some side effects, such as stomach upset or mood changes, can start straight away, while others such as a rounder face take weeks or months.

Doses

NHS says the usual dose varies from 5mg to 60mg a day, but sometimes higher doses are prescribed. The dose may go down as you improve, or go up if symptoms worsen. Unless told otherwise, take it as a single dose once a day with breakfast, so it is less likely to upset your stomach or keep you awake. Do not stop suddenly if you have taken it for more than 3 weeks. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists problems sleeping, weight gain, indigestion and mild mood changes as common. Side effects are more likely with higher doses or use over more than a few weeks.

Get help if: you have signs of infection (high temperature, sore throat, cough), mood changes such as feeling very high or very low, a very upset stomach with severe dizziness or fainting, or signs of a serious allergic reaction.

Extra care
  • Infections: NHS says tell your doctor if you have an infection or an unhealed wound, or have recently been in contact with chickenpox, shingles or measles.
  • Vaccinations: NHS says tell your doctor if you have had, or are about to have, a vaccination. BNF says live virus vaccines should be avoided at immunosuppressive (high) doses, because the body may not respond to them properly.
  • Diabetes and epilepsy: NHS says tell your doctor. BNF also lists high blood pressure, heart failure, glaucoma, tuberculosis (current or past), and a history of steroid-related muscle problems as reasons for care.
  • Painkillers: NHS says anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen and aspirin can add to stomach risk.
  • Steroid card: carry it if you take prednisolone for more than 3 weeks or on a high dose. Some people need a red steroid emergency card.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping suddenly after weeks of use or a high dose. This can cause withdrawal effects.
  • Taking it late in the day, which may keep you awake.
  • Not telling a doctor, dentist or pharmacist you take a steroid.
  • Mixing it up with propranolol. BNF warns the two names have been confused, so check the label every time.
  • Ignoring signs of infection, which steroids can hide or worsen.
Pregnancy

NHS says you can take prednisolone while pregnant. It has occasionally been linked to problems in the first 12 weeks and effects on the baby's growth, so the baby's growth will be checked. Speak to your doctor if you become pregnant. NHS says it is OK while breastfeeding if your doctor or health visitor says your baby is healthy. Small amounts pass into breast milk.
