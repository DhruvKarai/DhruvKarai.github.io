Ramipril

Tritace

ACE inhibitor: widens blood vessels by blocking the ACE enzyme

Key points

  • Used for high blood pressure and heart failure, and after a heart attack. NHS says it helps prevent future strokes, heart attacks and kidney problems.
  • It blocks the enzyme ACE, so blood vessels relax and widen.
  • Doctors start with a low dose and build up over a few weeks. NHS says the first dose can make you dizzy, so it may be taken at bedtime.
  • It is not recommended in pregnancy. Use contraception if you could get pregnant, and talk to your doctor first.

The details

How it works

Ramipril is a prodrug. In the body it is changed to ramiprilat, which blocks the angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE). ACE turns angiotensin I into angiotensin II, which narrows blood vessels and stimulates the hormone aldosterone. ACE also breaks down bradykinin, which widens blood vessels. With ACE blocked there is less angiotensin II and more bradykinin, and blood vessels widen. The SmPC says the response in Black (Afro-Caribbean) people with high blood pressure is on average lower.

How quickly

Ramipril is built up slowly. NHS says the starting dose is low, and it is raised gradually over a few weeks to the usual dose for your condition. Your doctor will check your blood pressure.

Doses

NHS says the usual starting dose is between 1.25mg and 2.5mg once a day, raised gradually to the usual dose for your condition. The maximum is 5mg twice a day or 10mg once a day. It is taken once or twice a day, with or without food (SmPC). Swallow it with liquid, and do not chew or crush it. You may have blood tests for your kidneys and potassium. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists a dry, persistent cough and feeling dizzy or lightheaded, especially when you stand up quickly, as common effects. Dizziness is more likely when you start or raise the dose.

Get help if: the whites of your eyes or your skin turn yellow, you have severe stomach pain, swollen ankles, blood in your pee or you are not peeing, or you have signs of a blood disorder (such as a sore throat, high temperature or easy bruising). NHS says call 999 or go to A&E for signs of a stroke, such as weakness on one side of the body.

Extra care
  • Pregnancy: not recommended. See the pregnancy section.
  • Kidney, liver or heart problems, low blood pressure, or dialysis: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Diabetes: NHS says check your blood sugar more often at first, because ramipril can lower it.
  • Other medicines: NHS says check before taking NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, potassium supplements, or potassium-sparing diuretics. NHS says daily low-dose aspirin (75mg) is safe with ramipril.
  • Diarrhoea or vomiting, a low-salt diet, or major surgery: NHS says tell your doctor.
Common mistakes
  • Standing up quickly after the first dose.
  • Taking painkillers such as ibuprofen without checking with a pharmacist.
  • Taking potassium supplements or potassium-sparing medicines without checking. NHS says they can raise your potassium.
  • Stopping because you feel well. High blood pressure often has no symptoms.
Pregnancy

NHS says ramipril is not recommended in pregnancy, because it can affect the baby's kidneys, especially in the second and third trimesters. If you are pregnant, stop taking it and talk to your doctor straight away. Use contraception while taking it, and plan any pregnancy with your doctor. The SmPC says it is contraindicated in the second and third trimesters, and not recommended in the first. Check with a doctor about breastfeeding.
