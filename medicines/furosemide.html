Furosemide

Loop diuretic

Loop diuretic ("water tablet"): helps the body get rid of extra fluid

Key points

  • A "water tablet" that makes you pee more, to clear extra fluid from the body.
  • Used for fluid build-up (oedema) from heart failure, kidney disease or severe liver damage.
  • Plan around it: you will need to pee more urgently and often, so take it in the morning.
  • If you are ill with vomiting, diarrhoea or a fever, you could become dehydrated. Ask for advice.

The details

How it works

Furosemide blocks a salt transporter (the sodium-potassium-chloride co-transporter) in part of the kidney tubule called the loop of Henle. This stops salt being taken back into the blood there. Salt stays in the tubule and water follows it, so you pass more urine. The SmPC calls it a potent, fast-acting diuretic.

How quickly

The SmPC says a prompt increase in urine usually follows, lasting about four hours after a dose. That is why the time of day you take it matters.

Doses

NHS says your doctor will usually start with a low dose and increase it after a week or more. You usually take it once a day at first, ideally in the morning. If you take it twice a day, take the second dose in the late afternoon. Take it with or without food, and swallow it with water. The SmPC gives, for oedema in adults, a starting dose of 40mg in the morning and a maintenance dose of 20mg daily or 40mg on alternate days. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists peeing more than usual, thirst with a dry mouth and lips (signs of dehydration), dizziness, headaches and muscle twitches as common effects.

Get help if: you have a swollen throat or tongue or a raised itchy rash, blistering skin with flu-like symptoms, back or side pain with a high temperature or blood in your pee, or you become very dehydrated (peeing much less, dark pee, dizziness, confusion).

Extra care
  • Kidney or liver problems, low blood pressure, diabetes, Addison's disease, or gout: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Illness: vomiting, diarrhoea or a high temperature can lead to dehydration (NHS).
  • Other medicines: NHS says some blood pressure medicines, heart rhythm medicines such as digoxin, potassium supplements, some NSAIDs, laxatives, lithium and some antidepressants and antipsychotics may not mix well with furosemide.
  • Alcohol: NHS says it is best not to drink, because it can cause dizziness, light-headedness and dehydration.
Common mistakes
  • Taking it late in the day. NHS says take it in the morning (and late afternoon if you take it twice a day).
  • Taking an extra dose. NHS says this can be dangerous.
  • Not drinking enough when you are ill with vomiting or diarrhoea.
  • Driving or cycling when dizzy.
Pregnancy

NHS says furosemide is not usually used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant, trying to get pregnant or breastfeeding. The SmPC says it crosses the placenta, passes into breast milk and may inhibit milk production, so it should be used only for compelling medical reasons.
