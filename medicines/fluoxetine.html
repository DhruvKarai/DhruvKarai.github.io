Fluoxetine

SSRI antidepressant

SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor): a type of antidepressant

Key points

  • An antidepressant from the SSRI group. Prescription only.
  • Used for depression, obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) and bulimia.
  • It can take a few weeks before you notice an improvement. Keep taking it as your doctor advises.
  • Do not stop it suddenly. Withdrawal symptoms can happen, so your doctor will reduce the dose gradually.

The details

How it works

Fluoxetine is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI). NHS says it is thought to work by increasing serotonin, a chemical in the brain linked to mood. The SmPC says it is a selective inhibitor of serotonin reuptake, which probably accounts for its mechanism of action, and that it has practically no affinity for other receptors.

How quickly

NHS says it can take a few weeks before your symptoms start to improve, and a lower starting dose may be increased after 3 or 4 weeks.

Doses

NHS says you usually take fluoxetine once a day, with or without food, at the same time each day. Doses depend on why you take it, and your doctor may start low and increase after 3 or 4 weeks. The SmPC recommends 20mg a day for major depression in adults, reviewed within 3 to 4 weeks. For depression you will probably need to keep taking it for several months or longer. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists headaches, feeling or being sick, diarrhoea or constipation, weight changes, sexual problems, problems sleeping, feeling dizzy or drowsy, and blurred vision as common effects. Some ease after a couple of weeks.

Get help if: you have thoughts about harming yourself or feel very low, you have signs of serotonin syndrome (fast heartbeat, sweating, shaking, twitching muscles, confusion or agitation), unexplained bruising or bleeding, or signs of a serious allergic reaction. NHS says if you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help straight away.

Extra care
  • Diabetes, epilepsy, heart disease or glaucoma: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Bleeding disorders and past mania: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Driving: NHS says do not drive, ride a bike or use machinery if you feel dizzy, drowsy or have blurred vision.
  • Children: NHS says it is sometimes used for depression in children aged 5 or over.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping suddenly. NHS says this may cause withdrawal symptoms.
  • Taking an extra dose, which NHS says can be dangerous.
  • Expecting benefit within days.
  • Not telling a doctor about mood changes such as feeling very high or energetic.
Pregnancy

NHS says fluoxetine can be used in pregnancy if needed. Your doctor will talk through risks and benefits and prescribe the lowest effective dose. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant. NHS says it is sometimes used while breastfeeding, but it passes into breast milk, so check with your doctor. They may switch you to another antidepressant such as sertraline.
