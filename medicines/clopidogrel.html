Clopidogrel

Grepid and Plavix

Antiplatelet: stops platelets sticking together to form clots

Key points

  • An antiplatelet that stops platelets (a type of blood cell) sticking together and forming a dangerous clot.
  • For people at higher risk of clots, for example after a heart attack, stroke or stent. Prescription only.
  • Usually one 75mg tablet once a day. Do not stop it without talking to your doctor.
  • The main side effect is bleeding more easily than normal. Do not take aspirin for pain or ibuprofen with it unless a doctor says so.

The details

How it works

Clopidogrel is a prodrug. The SmPC says the body must change it using CYP450 enzymes into an active metabolite. That metabolite blocks the platelet receptor P2Y12, so ADP can no longer switch platelets on, and they stick together less. Because the block is irreversible, affected platelets stay inhibited for the rest of their life, about 7 to 10 days.

How quickly

The SmPC describes a one-off higher loading dose (300mg or 600mg) when treatment starts in some situations. The effect on platelets lasts for the platelet's life, about 7 to 10 days, so it wears off slowly after stopping.

Doses

NHS says the usual dose is 75mg once a day, taken at the same time each day, swallowed whole with water, with or without food. Sometimes a one-off higher dose such as 300mg or 600mg is prescribed. You may need it for weeks, months or for life. Do not stop it without talking to your doctor. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists bleeding more easily than normal (nosebleeds, bruising, heavier periods), diarrhoea, and indigestion or heartburn as common effects.

Get help if: you have yellowing of the skin or eyes, have had a head injury, a nosebleed lasting more than 10 to 15 minutes, a sudden bad headache with confusion or slurred speech, or signs of a serious allergic reaction.

Extra care
  • Surgery or dental treatment: NHS says you may need to stop it for a short time, but check with your doctor or dentist first.
  • Other blood thinners and NSAIDs: NHS says tell your doctor if you take aspirin, warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban or NSAIDs such as ibuprofen.
  • Indigestion medicines: NHS says omeprazole and esomeprazole may affect how well clopidogrel works. Other antacids can be taken at the same time.
  • Grapefruit juice and St John's wort: NHS says grapefruit juice stops it working as well, and St John's wort can raise levels and the risk of bleeding.
  • Alcohol: NHS says you can drink, but not too much, as it can irritate your stomach.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping it without talking to your doctor. NHS says this raises the risk of heart attack or stroke.
  • Taking aspirin 300mg or ibuprofen for pain without asking.
  • Taking omeprazole or esomeprazole without telling your doctor.
  • Drinking grapefruit juice.
Pregnancy

NHS says clopidogrel is not often recommended in pregnancy but can be taken if needed, and is not thought to be harmful. Do not stop it unless advised. The SmPC says it is preferable not to use it in pregnancy as a precaution. NHS says you may be able to take it while breastfeeding if your baby is healthy, after talking to your doctor or pharmacist.
