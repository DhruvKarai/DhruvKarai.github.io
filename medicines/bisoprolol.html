Bisoprolol

Beta blocker

Beta blocker: slows the heart and lowers blood pressure

Key points

  • Used for high blood pressure, angina (heart-related chest pain) and, with other medicines, heart failure.
  • It blocks beta-1 receptors in the heart, so the heart beats more slowly and with less force.
  • Do not stop it suddenly. Your condition can get worse. Your doctor will reduce the dose gradually.
  • Prescription only. Tell your doctor if you have asthma or other breathing problems.

The details

How it works

Bisoprolol is a highly beta-1-selective blocker. Beta-1 receptors in the heart respond to adrenaline and noradrenaline. Blocking them lowers heart rate and the force of each beat. The SmPC says bisoprolol has only low affinity for beta-2 receptors in the airways and blood vessels, so it is generally not expected to affect airway resistance. Beta-2 effects still need care in people with asthma.

How quickly

NHS says your doctor may start with a low dose and raise it after around a week or more. This lowers the chance of side effects. Side effects usually happen when you start or raise the dose, and often get better over time.

Doses

Most people take it once a day, usually in the morning, swallowed whole with water. For high blood pressure and angina, the SmPC says the usual dose is 10mg once a day, starting at 5mg, with a maximum recommended dose of 20mg a day. Heart failure doses are raised in steps by a specialist. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists headaches, tiredness and weakness, cold, numb or tingling hands and feet, low blood pressure (dizziness), feeling or being sick, and diarrhoea or constipation as common effects.

Get help if: you have a swollen throat or tongue, a raised itchy rash, breathing problems, yellowing skin or eyes, or an unusual heartbeat. If you have heart failure, also get help if your heart symptoms get worse.

Extra care
  • Asthma or other breathing problems: NHS says tell your doctor first. BNF says beta blockers, including bisoprolol, should usually be avoided in asthma, and only used with specialist supervision if there is no alternative.
  • Low blood pressure, diabetes, kidney disease, or poor blood flow in the legs: NHS says tell your doctor. BNF notes that beta blockers can hide the warning signs of low blood sugar.
  • Other medicines: NHS says some heart medicines, blood pressure medicines, painkillers such as ibuprofen, some antidepressants and nose decongestants may not mix well with bisoprolol.
  • Alcohol and fasting: NHS says alcohol may increase the risk of low blood pressure, and fasting can affect your blood sugar.
Common mistakes
  • Stopping it suddenly because you feel well.
  • Taking two doses to make up for a missed one.
  • Starting nose decongestants or ibuprofen without checking.
  • Drinking alcohol and not expecting dizziness.
Pregnancy

NHS says bisoprolol can be taken in pregnancy, but you and your baby may be offered extra checks. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant. The SmPC says beta blockers can reduce blood flow to the placenta and cause problems in the baby, so treatment needs to be monitored. BNF says beta blockers may slow the baby's growth in the womb and cause low blood sugar and a slow heart rate in the newborn. NHS says taking bisoprolol while breastfeeding may cause side effects in the baby, but a doctor may recommend it if the benefits outweigh the risks.
