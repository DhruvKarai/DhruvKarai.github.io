Lisinopril

ACE inhibitor

ACE inhibitor: widens blood vessels by blocking the ACE enzyme

Key points

  • An ACE inhibitor that relaxes and widens blood vessels, lowering blood pressure.
  • Used for high blood pressure, after a recent heart attack, kidney problems caused by diabetes, and, with other medicines, heart failure.
  • A dry cough that does not go away is a common side effect. Dizziness is more likely when standing up quickly.
  • It is not usually used in pregnancy. Tell your doctor if you are pregnant or could become pregnant.

The details

How it works

Lisinopril inhibits the angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE). ACE turns angiotensin I into angiotensin II, which narrows blood vessels and stimulates the hormone aldosterone. With ACE blocked there is less angiotensin II and less aldosterone, so blood vessels widen and blood pressure falls. The SmPC notes that aldosterone falling can raise potassium in the blood.

How quickly

NHS says your doctor may start with a low dose and raise it gradually over a few days or weeks, which lowers the chance of side effects. The SmPC says peak blood levels occur about 7 hours after a dose.

Doses

NHS says lisinopril comes as tablets or liquid, taken at the same time each day, swallowed whole with water. The SmPC says it is a single daily dose, not affected by food, and the usual starting dose for high blood pressure is 10mg. Your doctor sets the dose from your blood pressure response, and you may have blood tests. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists headaches, feeling or being sick, diarrhoea, feeling dizzy (especially on standing up quickly), a dry cough that does not go away, and kidney problems (checked with blood tests) as common effects.

Get help if: you have swelling of the lips, tongue, face or throat (angioedema), signs of kidney failure (blood in your pee, passing much less pee, lower back pain), or signs of anaphylaxis. NHS says angioedema is more likely if you have black skin.

Extra care
  • Angioedema: NHS says tell your doctor if you have had swelling of the face, hands, feet, mouth or throat.
  • Kidney or liver problems: NHS says tell your doctor.
  • Other medicines: NHS says check if you take other blood pressure or heart medicines, diuretics, lithium, anticoagulants such as heparin, or painkillers such as ibuprofen.
  • Alcohol: NHS says it may be best to avoid it, because it can raise the risk of low blood pressure.
Common mistakes
  • Standing up quickly if you feel dizzy.
  • Taking ibuprofen or other painkillers without checking.
  • Putting up with a lasting dry cough without telling your doctor.
  • Taking two doses to make up for a missed one.
Pregnancy

NHS says lisinopril is not usually used in pregnancy because it can affect the baby's kidneys, skull, lungs and joints, reduce amniotic fluid and raise the risk of stillbirth. A doctor will recommend a safer alternative. The SmPC says it is not recommended in the first trimester and contraindicated after that. NHS says it may be taken while breastfeeding, but a doctor may suggest enalapril instead.
