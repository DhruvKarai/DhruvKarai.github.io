Tamsulosin

Contiflo XL, Flomax Relief and Tabphyn MR

Alpha blocker: relaxes muscle in the prostate and bladder outlet

Key points

  • An alpha blocker that relaxes muscle around the bladder and prostate so you can pee more easily.
  • Used by men with symptoms of an enlarged prostate. NHS says it is also occasionally used for kidney stones and prostatitis.
  • Take it once a day after breakfast, swallowed whole. It can make you dizzy when you stand up, so sit or lie down if that happens.
  • A common effect is less semen when you ejaculate. NHS says this is harmless.

The details

How it works

Tamsulosin binds selectively and competitively to alpha-1 adrenoceptors, mainly the alpha-1A and alpha-1D subtypes. The SmPC says this relaxes smooth muscle in the prostate and urethra, which reduces tension and improves urine flow. NHS says alpha blockers also relax blood vessels, which can lower blood pressure.

How quickly

NHS says take it in the morning after breakfast, because the highest levels of the medicine are in the body about 6 hours later. If you buy it from a pharmacy, NHS says see a GP within 6 weeks of starting.

Doses

NHS says the usual dose is 400 micrograms once a day, as slow-release capsules or tablets, swallowed whole with water while sitting or standing. Do not break, chew or crush them. Take it after breakfast or the first meal of the day. The SmPC says one capsule a day after breakfast or the first daily meal. For an enlarged prostate you can continue long term if it helps. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists feeling dizzy or sweating, especially when you sit or stand up, and problems ejaculating (little or no semen) as common effects. Many people have no side effects or only minor ones.

Get help if: you faint, have swelling under the skin or in the mouth, face, lips, tongue or throat (angioedema), or a painful erection lasting 2 hours or more. NHS says call 999 or go to A&E, and do not drive yourself.

Extra care
  • Low blood pressure on standing: NHS says tell a doctor if you have orthostatic hypotension, or ever faint while peeing.
  • Eye surgery: NHS says tell the doctor if you are having cataract or glaucoma surgery.
  • Severe liver or kidney problems: NHS says tell a doctor.
  • Blood pressure tablets: BNF says the dose of other blood pressure medicines may need adjusting, and the first dose needs extra care because of the risk of a drop in blood pressure on standing.
  • Alcohol: NHS says it can increase the blood pressure-lowering effect and make you dizzy or light-headed.
  • Driving: NHS says do not drive, ride a bike or use machinery if you feel dizzy.
Common mistakes
  • Breaking, chewing or crushing the slow-release capsules or tablets.
  • Standing up fast after a dose.
  • Stopping suddenly. NHS says your symptoms could come back.
  • Not mentioning it before cataract surgery.
Pregnancy

NHS says tamsulosin is mostly used by men. The NHS page says it is not usually recommended in pregnancy because there is not enough information to say whether it is safe. Talk to a doctor if you are pregnant or trying to get pregnant.
