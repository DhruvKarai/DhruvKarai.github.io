Metformin

Glucophage

Biguanide: lowers blood glucose

Key points

  • Lowers blood sugar in type 2 diabetes. NHS also lists gestational diabetes and polycystic ovary syndrome.
  • Usually taken with or after meals, 1 to 3 times a day. Swallow tablets whole.
  • On its own it does not cause low blood sugar.
  • If you are being sick, have diarrhoea or a fever, tell a doctor. Do not stop it without advice.

The details

How it works

Metformin lowers blood sugar mainly by reducing the glucose the liver makes and by helping body tissues use glucose better. It only works when the body still makes some insulin.

How quickly

The SmPC says blood levels peak about 2.5 hours after a dose. Doctors usually adjust the dose after 10 to 15 days based on blood glucose. NHS says side effects often settle within a couple of weeks.

Doses

A typical adult starting dose is 500mg or 850mg, 2 or 3 times a day with or after meals, adjusted by a doctor, up to a maximum of 3g a day (SmPC). NHS says most people take it 1 to 3 times a day. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

Feeling or being sick, diarrhoea, tummy pain, loss of appetite, a changed taste, and low vitamin B12 (which can cause tiredness). Most effects ease after a couple of weeks.

Get help if: side effects do not settle. See the NHS page for serious effects.

Extra care
  • May not suit people with liver or kidney problems, a severe infection, heart failure or heavy alcohol use.
  • Rare but serious: lactic acidosis. NHS says to call 999 for symptoms such as fast breathing, drowsiness, stomach pain or feeling very unwell.
  • Other medicines: NHS lists diuretics, anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen and some blood pressure medicines.
  • Long-term use can lower vitamin B12 (NHS).
Common mistakes
  • Taking it on an empty stomach if it upsets your stomach. It is taken with or after food.
  • Crushing or chewing tablets when they should be swallowed whole.
  • Doubling up after a missed dose. NHS says to skip it if the next dose is near.
  • Carrying on as normal during vomiting, diarrhoea or fever. NHS says dehydration is a risk, so tell a doctor.
  • Drinking a lot of alcohol. NHS says to limit it.
Pregnancy

NHS says metformin can usually be taken in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, but to check with a doctor first. The SmPC says it crosses the placenta, and that blood glucose should be kept as close to normal as possible in pregnancy.
