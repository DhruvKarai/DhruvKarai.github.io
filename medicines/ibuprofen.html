Ibuprofen

Nurofen

NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug): a painkiller that also treats inflammation

Key points

  • A painkiller that also reduces swelling and fever. It belongs to the NSAID family.
  • NHS suggests trying paracetamol first if you can take it, because ibuprofen causes more side effects.
  • Use the smallest dose for the shortest time. Do not take tablets or capsules for more than 10 days unless a doctor says so.
  • Not right for everyone. Check first if you have had stomach ulcers, or have asthma, or kidney, heart or liver problems.

The details

How it works

Ibuprofen blocks cyclo-oxygenase enzymes, so the body makes fewer prostaglandins. Prostaglandins drive pain, swelling and fever, so less of them means less of all three. Prostaglandins also do other jobs, such as helping keep blood flowing to the kidneys, so blocking them can cause side effects.

How quickly

Ibuprofen is absorbed quickly from the gut. Levels in the blood peak about 1 to 2 hours after a dose.

Doses

Typical adult 200mg tablets or capsules: 1 or 2 at a time, up to 3 times a day, at least 4 hours apart, with no more than 6 in 24 hours. Typical adult 400mg tablets or capsules: 1 at a time, up to 3 times a day, with no more than 3 in 24 hours. Swallow with water, ideally with or after food. Gels, sprays and liquids differ. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

Indigestion and stomach ache, feeling or being sick, headache, rash, dizziness, diarrhoea or constipation. Gel and spray cause fewer side effects.

Get help if: you have signs of a stomach bleed or a serious allergic reaction. See the NHS page for the full list.

Extra care
  • Stomach: do not use it if you have or have had a stomach ulcer. Ibuprofen can cause ulcers and bleeding, and the risk is higher with high doses or long use.
  • Kidneys, heart and liver: NHS says it may not suit people with kidney, heart or liver conditions, or a past stroke. The SmPC lists people at greatest risk of kidney problems as those with kidney, heart or liver problems, those taking water tablets (diuretics) or ACE inhibitors, and older people.
  • Asthma and allergies: NHS says check first if you have asthma or allergies such as hay fever. The SmPC says people with asthma should ask a doctor before using it.
  • Chickenpox and clotting: NHS says it may not suit you if you have chickenpox or a blood-clotting problem.
  • Other medicines: NHS says some do not mix well with ibuprofen, including blood thinners such as warfarin, steroids, some antidepressants and some blood pressure medicines.
Common mistakes
  • Taking it with another NSAID such as naproxen or aspirin. This raises the risk of stomach ulcers.
  • Taking it on an empty stomach. NHS says it is best taken with or after food.
  • Carrying on for more than 10 days (tablets and capsules) without a doctor's advice.
  • Taking extra for stronger pain relief. Higher doses and longer use raise the risk of serious side effects.
  • Using it without checking when you have asthma, a stomach ulcer, kidney problems or chickenpox.
Pregnancy

NHS says to avoid ibuprofen in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist advises it, and that paracetamol is usually the best painkiller. The SmPC says it should not be used in the first or second trimester unless clearly necessary, and from the 20th week it can cause kidney problems in the baby. It may also affect fertility in women, so tell a pharmacist or doctor if you are trying to get pregnant. When breastfeeding, NHS says paracetamol is usually best, but ibuprofen can often be used if needed. Check with a pharmacist or doctor first.
