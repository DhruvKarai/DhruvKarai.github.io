Warfarin

Anticoagulant

Anticoagulant ("blood thinner"): makes the blood slower to clot

Key points

  • An anticoagulant, often called a blood thinner, that helps prevent and treat blood clots.
  • You need regular INR blood tests, so your doctor can check you are on the right dose.
  • The main side effect is bleeding. Carry your anticoagulant alert card.
  • Many medicines, foods and drinks affect it. Check before starting anything new, including herbal remedies.

The details

How it works

Warfarin is a vitamin K antagonist. The SmPC says it works by inhibiting the making of the vitamin K-dependent clotting factors II, VII, IX and X, and of the anticoagulant proteins C and S. Vitamin K is needed for the body to make these factors, so with less of them the blood takes longer to clot. It is not a "thinner" in the literal sense of making blood watery.

How quickly

The SmPC says the effect builds up gradually, because the clotting factors it reduces have half-lives from a few hours to about 2 to 3 days. That is why dosing is adjusted using blood tests, especially at the start.

Doses

Dose is individual and changes with your INR results. NHS says when you first start you need blood tests every day or every few days, and then usually about every 12 weeks once stable. It is taken once a day at the same time, with or without food. The SmPC gives a maintenance range of 3 to 9mg a day, set by the prescriber from your INR. Your doctor gives you a treatment record. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS says the main side effect is an increased risk of bleeding.

Get help if: you have blood in your pee or poo, unexplained bruising or nosebleeds, you cough up or vomit blood, you have a head injury, or a sudden bad headache with confusion or slurred speech. NHS also lists a painful skin rash (calciphylaxis) and anaphylaxis as serious effects.

Extra care
  • Alert card: NHS says carry your anticoagulant alert card and show it to your doctor or dentist before procedures.
  • Other medicines: NHS says NSAIDs such as ibuprofen or aspirin, antibiotics, antidepressants, heart medicines, stomach ulcer medicines, antifungals and statins may not mix well.
  • Herbal remedies and supplements: NHS says do not take St John's wort or vitamin K supplements.
  • Food and drink: NHS says do not drink large amounts of alcohol, and avoid cranberry juice, cranberry products and grapefruit juice.
  • Conditions: NHS says tell your doctor about liver or kidney problems, high blood pressure, a recent stroke or surgery, or a stomach ulcer.
Common mistakes
  • Starting a new medicine, supplement or herbal remedy without checking.
  • Taking two doses to make up for a missed one. NHS says skip it if you do not remember until the next day.
  • Missing INR blood tests.
  • Drinking cranberry or grapefruit juice, or large amounts of alcohol.
Pregnancy

NHS says warfarin is not usually used in pregnancy because it can affect the baby's development, but it can sometimes be used. Tell your doctor straight away if you are pregnant or planning to be, and do not stop it unless your doctor says so. NHS says warfarin can be taken while breastfeeding.
