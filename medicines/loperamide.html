Loperamide

Imodium

Antimotility medicine: slows down the gut to treat diarrhoea

Key points

  • A medicine that slows the gut down to stop diarrhoea. It treats the symptom, not the cause.
  • You can buy it from pharmacies for short-term diarrhoea in anyone aged 12 or over.
  • Stop as soon as symptoms settle. Do not take it for more than 48 hours without talking to a doctor.
  • Drink plenty of fluids, because diarrhoea can dehydrate you.

The details

How it works

Loperamide is a synthetic opioid that works in the gut. It binds to opioid receptors in the gut wall, which slows gut movement and may also reduce fluid secretion into the gut. It also increases the tone of the anal sphincter. Very little reaches the rest of the body: the SmPC gives the amount that reaches the bloodstream as about 0.3%, because most is cleared by the liver on the first pass.

How quickly

The SmPC says the anti-diarrhoea effect can start within an hour of a 4mg dose.

Doses

Adults: NHS says the usual starting dose is 2 capsules or tablets (4mg) straight away, then 1 after each runny poo. Stop when symptoms settle. The maximum in 24 hours is 6 capsules or tablets if bought from a shop or pharmacy, or 8 if prescribed. Do not take it for more than 48 hours without talking to a doctor. Children aged 11 and under: only on prescription. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists constipation, feeling sick, headaches and wind (farting) as common effects. If you find it difficult to poo, stop taking loperamide.

Get help if: you feel faint or less alert, move in a clumsy or uncoordinated way, have stomach pain with a high temperature or fast pulse, get severe constipation, or have difficulty peeing.

Extra care
  • Do not take it if you have severe diarrhoea after antibiotics, a flare-up of ulcerative colitis, or you are constipated or your tummy looks swollen (NHS).
  • Blood in your poo with a high temperature can be a sign of dysentery. Talk to a doctor first (NHS).
  • Diarrhoea for more than 48 hours, liver problems, or HIV with a swollen tummy: talk to a doctor first (NHS).
  • If you have IBS and you are 40 or over, notice blood in your poo, have lost weight or have recently travelled abroad, talk to a doctor first (NHS).
Common mistakes
  • Taking it for more than 48 hours without speaking to a doctor.
  • Taking more than the maximum for 24 hours.
  • Using it for children aged 11 or under without a prescription.
  • Forgetting to drink enough fluid while you have diarrhoea.
Pregnancy

NHS says loperamide is not usually recommended in pregnancy because there is not enough information to say whether it is safe. Talk to a doctor before taking it. NHS says it is OK while breastfeeding, because only tiny amounts reach breast milk.
