Sertraline

Key points

  • An antidepressant (SSRI). Used for depression, OCD, panic disorder, PTSD and social anxiety.
  • Usually taken once a day at the same time, with or without food.
  • It can take time to work. Do not stop suddenly.
  • If you have thoughts of harming yourself, get help straight away. Call 111 or see a doctor.

The details

How it works

Sertraline is a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI). It slows the recycling of serotonin back into nerve cells, so more serotonin stays available. Serotonin is a brain chemical linked to mood, and that change is thought to ease symptoms over a few weeks.

How quickly

The SmPC says an effect may be seen within 7 days, but longer is usually needed. Blood levels peak 4.5 to 8.4 hours after a dose.

Doses

NHS says you usually start on a low dose, and a doctor may raise it after a week or more. The SmPC gives 50mg a day to start for depression and OCD (25mg for panic disorder, PTSD and social anxiety), raised in steps at intervals of at least a week, up to 200mg a day. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

Headache, feeling sick, dizziness or drowsiness, dry mouth, diarrhoea, sleep problems, lower sex drive and weight gain. Many ease after a couple of weeks.

Get help if: side effects are severe or you feel worse. Do not stop suddenly without medical advice. See the NHS page.

Extra care
  • May not suit people with diabetes, epilepsy, heart disease, glaucoma, bleeding disorders or past mania.
  • Serious: serotonin syndrome, mania and thoughts of self-harm. NHS says to call 111 if you have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Other medicines: NHS lists anticoagulants such as warfarin and apixaban, anti-inflammatory painkillers, MAOIs and St John's wort.
  • It is best to avoid alcohol (NHS).
Common mistakes
  • Stopping suddenly. NHS says this can cause withdrawal symptoms, and the dose is reduced gradually.
  • Expecting an effect in a day or two.
  • Taking St John's wort with it. NHS says not to.
  • Doubling up after a missed dose. NHS says to skip it.
  • Driving or using machines if you feel dizzy or drowsy.
Pregnancy

NHS says sertraline can be used in pregnancy if needed, at the lowest effective dose, and to tell a doctor or midwife. It says breastfeeding is often fine, but to check first. The SmPC says a lot of data showed no malformations, that newborns can have withdrawal-like symptoms, and that small amounts pass into breast milk.
