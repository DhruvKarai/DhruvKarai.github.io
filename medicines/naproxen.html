Naproxen

NSAID painkiller

NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug): a painkiller that also treats inflammation

Key points

  • A painkiller that also reduces swelling (inflammation). It belongs to the NSAID family, like ibuprofen and aspirin.
  • Used for period pain, joint and muscle pain, gout, osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis.
  • Take the smallest dose for the shortest time, ideally with or after food. If bought without a prescription, do not take it for longer than 3 days.
  • Do not take it with ibuprofen, aspirin or another NSAID. It can cause stomach ulcers and bleeding.

The details

How it works

Naproxen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug. The SmPC says it inhibits prostaglandin synthetase, as other NSAIDs do. Prostaglandins are chemicals involved in pain, swelling and fever, so there are fewer of them. The SmPC also says that, as with other NSAIDs, the exact mechanism of its anti-inflammatory action is not known.

How quickly

The SmPC says naproxen is completely absorbed from the gut, with peak blood levels reached in 2 to 4 hours. The half-life is 12 to 15 hours, which is why it is usually taken up to 3 times a day or less.

Doses

NHS says naproxen is usually taken up to 3 times a day, and the dose depends on the type and why you take it. Swallow tablets whole with water, ideally with or after food. Do not chew or crush gastro-resistant or film-coated tablets. Your doctor may add a stomach-protecting medicine such as omeprazole. These are typical adult values; if your packet or prescriber says something different, follow that. These are typical adult doses, not personal advice.

Side effects

NHS lists constipation or diarrhoea, dizziness, feeling sleepy or tired, bloating and stomach pain, headaches, feeling or being sick, a rash and ringing in the ears (tinnitus) as common effects.

Get help if: you have signs of a stomach ulcer or bleed (tummy pain, black sticky poo, vomiting blood), kidney problems (blood in your pee, swollen ankles), heart failure symptoms, or signs of a serious allergic reaction. The risk is higher with a high dose or long use.

Extra care
  • Stomach: NHS says naproxen may not be suitable if you have, or have had, a stomach ulcer, or have Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis.
  • Heart, liver or kidney conditions, a past stroke, and blood-clotting problems: NHS says check first.
  • Asthma or allergies such as hay fever: NHS says check first.
  • Other medicines: NHS says some do not mix well with naproxen, including warfarin, steroids such as prednisolone, antidepressants such as citalopram, water tablets such as furosemide, and methotrexate.
  • Alcohol: NHS says limit it, because it can raise the risk of stomach ulcers and bleeding.
Common mistakes
  • Taking it with ibuprofen, aspirin or another NSAID. NHS says this raises the risk of stomach ulcers.
  • Taking it on an empty stomach. NHS says ideally take it with or after food.
  • Taking it for more than 3 days when bought without a prescription.
  • Chewing or crushing gastro-resistant or film-coated tablets.
Pregnancy

NHS says to avoid naproxen in pregnancy unless a doctor or pharmacist advises it, and that paracetamol is usually the best painkiller. The SmPC says it is contraindicated in the last trimester. NHS says it is not usually used while breastfeeding, and that other NSAIDs such as ibuprofen are safer, although a doctor may recommend it if the benefits outweigh the risks.
